Retinomorphic Vision Sensors

Kwabena Boahen (Stanford University and Institute for Advanced Study)
Friday, November 13, 2026, 10:30 am · Dilworth Room, Simons Hall, Institute for Advanced Study

Instead of capturing images at a fixed rate, like conventional frame cameras do, event cameras measure their pixels’ luminance changes and asynchronously output a stream of “address-events” that encode a luminance change location, time, and sign. These cameras offer a higher sampling rate (10 kHz vs. 60 Hz), shorter latency (40 μs vs. 16.7 ms), and higher dynamic range (140 dB vs. 60 dB) than frame cameras. Efficient and reliable event cameras have been widely available since 2008, when the Dynamic Vision Sensor (DVS) concept, inspired by luminance adaptation in photoreceptors, was developed.

I will describe a contemporaneously developed Retinomorphic Vision Sensor (RVS), inspired by spatiotemporal filtering and contrast adaptation in the vertebrate retina’s downstream circuitry. This preprocessing solves the problem of temporal frequency confounding spatial derivatives with speed by accepting only a narrow band of spatial frequencies and adapting the temporal filter’s corner-frequency to the temporal frequencies that motion produces. Thus, it transforms uniform optical flow across an object into coherent neural responses across space. That allows the retina to segregate a moving target from a cluttered background by detecting incoherent changes in luminance induced by their independent movement.
